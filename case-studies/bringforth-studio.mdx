## Context

I founded Bringforth Studio to solve a problem I kept hitting in B2B sales. You'd get a list of domains and then the real work started: clicking through half-broken About pages, looking for signs that a company fit your ICP. Hours of it. Days of it. Smart people doing the work a slow, expensive scraper could do.

I was the founder and product manager on this project. We built it for our own agency and eventually for clients.

---

## Problem / Product Goal

Manual prospecting is the biggest bottleneck in the sales cycle. You're asking talented people to act like robots, and robots would be better at it.

The core tension was simple: you need high-intent leads, but finding them requires understanding nuance. Does this company actually have the problem we solve? Do they have the budget? Or do they just happen to use the right keywords on their website? A human can answer those questions, but after the 400th site, they start missing details. An AI doesn't blink, but most automation tools are too dumb to look past surface-level signals.

The goal was to take raw, noisy domain lists and turn them into qualified leads automatically — without losing the human judgment that makes outreach effective.

---

## Thought Process

I started with something simple: scrape websites, pull the text, and run keyword matching. It worked for the obvious cases — if the word "SaaS" appeared, flag it. But it was useless for anything nuanced. A company could be a perfect fit and never mention the right keywords. A company could mention them and be totally wrong.

So I moved to LLM-based qualification. Instead of looking for strings, I'd feed the full page content into an LLM and ask it to make a judgment call. Does this company's service offerings align with our solution? Do their case studies suggest they have the budget? It worked way better than keyword matching, but it introduced a new problem: you can't judge a business by its meta description. You need the whole picture — service pages, about page, case studies, tech stack mentions.

That meant I needed proper browser rendering. Most modern sites are JavaScript-heavy messes. BeautifulSoup-style HTTP scraping returns nothing but a loading spinner. So I containerized headless browsers with Docker. Each site gets a full page render — JavaScript, pop-ups, bot detection all handled before we extract the content.

The pipeline became: domain list → Dockerized browser renders each site → structured extraction → LLM qualification → scored lead list. The human only comes in at the end for a final review.

---

## Solution

Three layers, each solving a specific bottleneck.

**High-Performance Backend (Python + FastAPI).** Processing thousands of domains means orchestrating hundreds of LLM calls alongside browser automation. FastAPI's async model handles the concurrency well. A list that would take a human weeks gets processed in minutes.

**Dockerized Headless Browsing.** Each target site gets a full browser render inside a container. Handles the JavaScript, the pop-ups, the anti-bot friction. What comes out is clean, structured page content — not a loading spinner or a "we detected automation" wall.

**LLM-Based Qualification.** The qualification layer reads the extracted content holistically. It's not checking for keywords. It's asking: does this company have the budget? Do they have the pain points? Are they a good fit? The difference between a regex search and a junior analyst doing business analysis.

The human's job shrinks from "click through every site and decide" to "review the scored list and give the final yes or no."

---

## Takeaways

**70% reduction in manual work** is the headline number, but the real win was different. An 8-hour workday of prospecting condensed to a 15-minute review. That changes how you think about your pipeline. You can test messaging against larger lists, explore adjacent markets, run experiments that wouldn't be worth the time manually.

**AI doesn't replace judgment, it extends attention.** The system doesn't make the final call — it pre-qualifies so a human can apply their judgment where it matters most. The mistake would have been trying to automate the whole thing end-to-end. Keeping the human in the loop was the right call.

**Architecture mirrors use case.** The whole system was structured around a simple idea: the use case is building relationships. Everything else — scraping, sorting, data entry — is infrastructure. When you start from the use case instead of the technology, the architecture practically designs itself.
